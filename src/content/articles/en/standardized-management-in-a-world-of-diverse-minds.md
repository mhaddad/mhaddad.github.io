In 1911, Frederick Winslow Taylor published *The Principles of Scientific Management*, the book that would shape the way work is organized for more than a century. The central idea was simple and powerful: break work down into elementary tasks, time each one, identify the "one best way" to perform them and replicate that way at scale. Industrial productivity multiplied. The model spread. And a silent premise became naturalized in modern organizations: people are interchangeable.

The premise is so deeply internalized that it is rarely examined. When a company redesigns a process, it looks for the optimal method, the one that will work for whoever ends up in the role. When it hires, it looks for the "ideal profile" for the position. When it evaluates performance, it compares each person against a single standard of excellence. In each of these moments, the Taylorist legacy operates in the background: there is a right way to do the work, and management's role is to identify that way and make sure everyone follows it.

The vocabulary has changed over time. "Human resources" became "human capital," then "people and culture." "Employees" became "collaborators," then "talent." Organizations talk about purpose, diversity, autonomy and innovation. But the underlying mechanical architecture remains, to a large extent, intact. Companies keep treating people as interchangeable parts in an optimizable system, even if with a more sophisticated discourse about human value.

That is the contradiction that motivated this series of articles.

## The contemporary paradox

Modern organizations today live with a structural tension between what they declare and what they practice. In their discourse, they value diversity. In practice, they standardize management models, evaluation processes and career tracks. In their discourse, they talk about autonomy. In practice, they keep deep layers of approval, control and supervision. In their discourse, they celebrate each person's uniqueness. In practice, they apply the same onboarding, the same meeting cadence, the same team rituals and the same performance criteria to completely different cognitive profiles.

The cost of this contradiction is high, and it tends to be invisible because it is attributed to the person, not to the organizational design.

When a person with high autonomy gets stuck in a rigid hierarchical structure, we call it "failure to adapt." When a person who needs structure breaks down in an environment of radical self-management, we call it "professional immaturity."

When an **Analytical Specialist** seems "cold" in mandatory team-building meetings, we call it a "soft skills problem." When a **Creative Innovator** doesn't finish the administrative projects assigned to them, we call it a "lack of commitment." In each of these cases, what is wrong is not the person, it is the design of the work.

The paradox is complete when you notice that the very organizations that standardize management models are the ones that suffer most from rates of disengagement, turnover and burnout that have repeated themselves for decades. Gallup's *State of the Global Workplace* report shows, year after year, that about two-thirds of workers around the world are not engaged. Organizations respond with more culture programs, more climate surveys and more leadership training. And the numbers don't move.

Maybe that's because the problem isn't where they are looking.

## The fallacy of universal self-management

My critique of standardization is not a defense of modern management models against traditional ones. Modern models, when applied without considering Cognitive Coherence, also produce suffering. Self-management at scale, in most of the organizations where it was implemented without a diagnosis, generated as much dysfunction as the hierarchical models it set out to replace. The difference is that the suffering changed shape.

In **hierarchical organizations**, people with high readiness for autonomy atrophy because their capacities are underused. The symptom is silent disengagement, boredom and early departure for companies that offer more room (when this scenario doesn't turn into a prison, like a cage with water and birdseed for a bird).

In **self-managed organizations**, people who need structure go into overload and anxiety. The symptom is the search for informal bosses, frozen decisions and exhaustion from too much lateral coordination.

[Doblinger (2023)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1198196/full), applying Self-Determination Theory to the study of distributed organizations, empirically documented what he called a critical autonomy threshold: when the autonomy offered exceeds each individual's ideal autonomy, engagement starts to fall instead of rising.

The lesson is hard to accept, because it goes against the ideological impulse to defend one model as universally superior. Even so, it is an unavoidable lesson: **there is no management model that is right for everyone**. There is coherence or incoherence between individual cognitive architectures and organizational architectures. The intelligence of those who design organizations lies in recognizing this plurality and building environments where different profiles can find their Places of Potential.

## Recognizing cognitive diversity is not a concession

There is a common resistance to this kind of proposal. It shows up in arguments like "if everyone works their own way, it turns into anarchy," or "you can't customize the environment for each person," or even "the company needs efficiency, not individual comfort." These arguments start from a mistaken premise: that recognizing cognitive diversity means making concessions to personal sensitivities. It is something else. It is recognizing a structural reality of how humans function, one that industrial standardization has always treated as noise and that is now starting to be treated as signal.

Doyle (2020) and the [CIPD's *Neuroinclusion at Work* report (2024)](https://www.cipd.org/globalassets/media/knowledge/knowledge-hub/reports/2024-pdfs/2024-neuroinclusion-at-work-report-8545.pdf) documented that organizations that redesign environments to accommodate different processing profiles are not just being "inclusive." They are **capturing performance that conventional design wastes**. The organizational literature points in the same direction: teams with real cognitive diversity, not just demographic diversity, produce more innovation, make better decisions on complex problems and show greater resilience under uncertainty. The condition for this to happen is that the work environment be designed to let different profiles contribute in different ways, instead of requiring everyone to contribute in the same way.

This changes what we mean by equity at work. Equity is not giving the same thing to everyone. It is recognizing that different people need different conditions to deliver fully. Treating differences as if they didn't exist produces inequality in practice, because it silently privileges a single cognitive profile (the one that matches the average design of the environment) and pushes everyone else to the margins.

Recognizing cognitive diversity is, therefore, both an ethical imperative and a performance strategy. The two dimensions go hand in hand.

## The humanist argument

There is an even deeper argument that deserves to be made explicit in the conceptual close of this series.

People spend roughly a third of their adult lives at work. That time is lived with some degree of coherence or incoherence with the way each person thinks, decides and coordinates with the world. When there is coherence, work is a source of purpose, learning, connection and growth. When there is sustained incoherence, work is a source of wear, anxiety, loss of self-confidence and, eventually, illness.

Designing work environments without considering the cognitive diversity of the people who inhabit them is a silent form of neglect. A discreet neglect, with the appearance of administrative neutrality or social commitment, but with deep cumulative effects on the mental health and sense of dignity of millions of people.

Every **Creative Innovator** fired for "lack of organization" is a loss. Every **Analytical Specialist** who burns out because of "difficulty with relationships" is a loss. Every **Flow Coordinator** paralyzed by anxiety in a self-management environment without support is a loss. For the people, first and foremost. For the organizations, as an inevitable consequence.

My P-O Fit proposal, in the end, is simple: to make visible what has always been invisible. To give a name to the compatibility or incompatibility between cognitive architectures and work environments. To offer a common vocabulary that allows people and organizations to talk about *fit* with the precision the subject deserves, instead of treating it as intuition, luck or personal affinity.

## Wrapping up the series (almost)

It took seven articles to present one idea: **people and organizations are compatible or incompatible depending on the Cognitive Coherence between distinct architectures, and this compatibility can be measured, described and used to design work environments where more people thrive and fewer people get sick**.

We started with the observation that excellent people don't always manage to work well in certain environments, and that this is not only their problem. We went through the definition of Cognitive Coherence as a functional compatibility between architectures. We explored the two dimensions of this compatibility in depth: readiness for autonomy and orientation toward interpersonal coordination. We presented the four indices that make these dimensions measurable. We mapped the 16 Places of Potential. We described the four onboarding models derived from these profiles. And we arrived here, at the broader argument: recognizing this diversity is not fussiness, it is an ethical and also a strategic necessity.

I hope something of what was written here has been useful, whether in understanding your own professional path or in designing the organization where you work. And if any of the concepts presented echoed a real experience you lived through or observed, I would love to hear about it.

I'll show how the tool was built, what it delivers and how you can contribute to the ongoing refinement of the model.

## Something to reflect on

Looking back on your professional path, at what moments did you feel most aligned with the environment where you were working? What did those environments have in common, and what did you learn about yourself in those experiences?

And looking ahead, what work environment would you like to inhabit in the coming years? Do you know, clearly, what you need in it for your way of thinking, deciding and coordinating with others to operate at its fullest?
