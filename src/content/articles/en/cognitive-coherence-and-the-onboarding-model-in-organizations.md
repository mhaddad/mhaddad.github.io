What was still missing was to show how this diagnosis translates into a concrete organizational decision. The most immediate and most underestimated of them is **onboarding**, the process through which a new person is integrated into the organization in their first weeks and months of work.

## The post-hiring psychological contract

Organizational literature has documented for decades that a person's first 90 days in a new organization determine, to a large extent, their long-term engagement. Bauer and Erdogan (2011), reviewing 30 years of research on organizational socialization, showed that **the role clarity, trust in colleagues and sense of belonging built during onboarding predict retention, performance and satisfaction up to 18 months later**. Schein (1971) had argued, even earlier, that onboarding is where the **psychological contract** is either sealed or broken.

Despite this centrality, most organizations treat onboarding as an administrative process. There is a list of tasks to complete, a series of people to meet, a few documents to sign. The list is the same for everyone who comes in. It doesn't matter whether the person is a **Creative Innovator**, an **Analytical Specialist** or an **Operations Coordinator**: they will go through the same sequence of meetings, trainings and social rituals.

The problem with this approach is structural, and it becomes evident when you examine what standard onboarding assumes about the person who is arriving.

## The imaginary profile of standard onboarding

Standardized onboarding implicitly assumes a specific cognitive architecture: someone who learns well in a linear sequence, tolerates many interruptions to meet colleagues, processes information better in synchronous meetings, values social rituals as a mechanism of belonging and prefers predictable structure to an open challenge. This profile exists, and it is probably the one closest to the **Connections Coordinator** or the **Flow Coordinator** in the P-O Fit model.

For all of the other 14 profiles, standard onboarding will generate some level of discomfort, friction or wasted potential. A **Strategic Intrapreneur** will be bored by the second week because they still haven't been given a real, complex problem to solve. An **Analytical Specialist** will be exhausted in the first week because they were forced to sit through endless meetings when what they needed was time to read and silence. A **Process Specialist** will be anxious the whole time because nobody gave them a clear script of what they are supposed to do each day.

The most serious consequence is that these signs of friction tend to be interpreted as "individual problems." The **Intrapreneur** seems "arrogant" for not engaging with the introductory meetings. The **Analytical Specialist** seems "antisocial" for not joining the welcome lunches. The **Process Specialist** seems "insecure" for asking for so many instructions. None of these judgments is true. All of them are the consequence of a process design that is incompatible with the cognitive architecture of the person being integrated.

## What onboarding should do

Before presenting the models, it is worth redefining what onboarding should deliver.

The purpose of onboarding is not to "acculturate" the person. It is not to make them "wear the company jersey." It is not even to introduce them to all of their colleagues. The purpose is **to create the environmental conditions for them to reach their Place of Potential as quickly as possible, with the least unnecessary friction between their cognitive architecture and the organizational model** they are getting to know.

Each profile has a different path to that Place. For some, the path involves receiving radical autonomy from day one. For others, it involves receiving structure and predictability until they develop enough confidence to exercise autonomy. For some, it involves intense social immersion. For others, it involves silence and deep work.

Recognizing this diversity is what allows the P-O Fit model to recommend not a single integration track, but four distinct models, each one designed for a coherent set of cognitive profiles.

## Model A — Immersion in Autonomy

**For the profiles:** Systemic Facilitator, Facilitating Leader, Strategic Intrapreneur, High-Impact Executor.

These profiles combine high readiness for autonomy with high resilience. For them, onboarding needs to be short and focused on context and purpose. They need to understand the governance rules, the history of relevant decisions and the organization's strategic challenges. Anything beyond that is perceived as paternalism.

The ideal duration is 1 to 2 weeks, the shortest of the four models. In the very first week, the person should be given full access to organizational information and a real, complex problem to solve. The idea is that they navigate the organization to find the answers, and that this navigation is, in itself, the onboarding.

The mentor (or *buddy*) acts as a facilitator of connections, not as an instructor. They remove bureaucratic obstacles, make strategic introductions and then get out of the way. Rituals are minimal: a check-in at the end of the first week and another at the end of the second. The individual sets the pace.

The success criterion is simple: the person is operating autonomously and making a real contribution by the end of the second week. The main risk is underutilization. If the problem offered is too simple, engagement plummets immediately.

## Model B — Structure and Safety

**For the profiles:** Flow Coordinator, Support Coordinator, Sustainment Specialist, Process Specialist.

These profiles need support: structure, predictability and gradual exposure to autonomy. The focus of onboarding is to reduce the anxiety of the unknown and to build, week after week, the confidence that the organization's culture is safe. When the company has traits of self-management, the transition into those traits should be slow and sustained.

The ideal duration is 60 to 90 days, the longest of the four models. The first week has a completely predictable agenda: manuals, process documentation, work scripts and an introduction to the fixed rituals. The person should know exactly what is expected of them each day. In the following weeks, the goals are short (daily or weekly), validated by the mentor, with a gradual introduction to autonomous decisions, starting with low-impact ones.

The mentor acts as a "culture translator." They help the person understand that they can decide and that mistakes will not be punished when they are transparent. This is a critical point: the mentor needs to resist the temptation to become an "informal boss," because the goal is to build autonomy, not dependence. They should do less each week, not more.

The success criterion, at the end of the period, is a visible reduction in the initial anxiety and the development of the ability to decide within one's own scope without constant external validation.

## Model C — Low-Noise Integration

**For the profiles:** Autonomous Specialist, Analytical Specialist, Compliance Specialist, Integrity Specialist.

These profiles thrive on deep, independent work. Onboarding should prioritize asynchronous communication, silence and integration through competence, not through socializing. The person feels part of the group through the value they generate, not through social rituals.

The ideal duration is 3 to 4 weeks. Integration in the first week happens mostly through reading: technical documentation, access to tools, repositories and internal wikis. Marathons of social welcome meetings are contraindicated. A brief introductory meeting (30 minutes) is enough; the rest should be asynchronous.

From the second week on, the individual is already operating in their technical domain. The mentor steps in only when asked or for scheduled technical feedback, usually weekly and in writing. The primary communication channel is text: Discord, Slack, Notion, email, etc. Synchronous meetings are reduced to the essentials and always have an agenda set in advance.

The mentor acts as a technical peer. They respect the person's concentration time and offer literal, evidence-based feedback. They are not a social facilitator: they are someone who knows the domain and is available when needed.

The success criterion is quality delivery in the technical domain within the first few weeks, with functioning asynchronous channels. The person doesn't need to "feel at home." They need to feel productive.

## Model D — Bonding and Rituals Integration

**For the profiles:** Team Orchestrator, Creative Innovator, Operations Coordinator, Connections Coordinator.

These profiles flourish on human connection. For them, the "who" comes before the "what": performance only shows up after belonging has been consolidated. Onboarding should be rich in social rituals, cross-introductions and building a relational network.

The ideal duration is 3 to 4 weeks. The first week is a relational immersion: cross-introductions between teams, sessions for sharing personal purpose, lunches with different members of the team. The individual should meet as many people as possible right at the start, in person or by video. The agenda is intentionally social.

From the second week on, the person actively takes part in the organization's rituals (stand-ups, retrospectives, governance sessions). The focus is for them to observe, participate and begin to understand the informal dynamics, that is, who interacts with whom, how decisions are actually made, where the latent tensions are. By week 4, ideally, they are already contributing with their relational competence: facilitating alignment, mediating small conflicts, integrating other new people.

The mentor is social and relational. They focus on the organization's network of relationships: who is who, how each person likes to collaborate, what the informal dynamics are that nobody ever writes down anywhere. The goal is for the newcomer to build their own network of trust quickly.

Rituals are abundant. This is the model that invests most in rituals, because, for these profiles, rituals are the mechanism of integration, not a process cost.

## The logic behind the mapping

Looking at the four onboarding models, you can see the logic of the mapping between profiles and models. It is not a mapping by quadrant (Q1 → Model X, Q2 → Model Y). It is a mapping by **the cross combination of readiness and social orientation**.

Profiles with high readiness for autonomy that don't need structure get Model A. Profiles that need environmental support get Model B. Profiles oriented toward deep, independent work get Model C. Profiles oriented toward human connection get Model D.

That is why, within the same quadrant, different profiles can get different models. In Q1, the Systemic Facilitator and the Facilitating Leader go to Model A, while the Team Orchestrator and the Creative Innovator go to Model D. The first two have high agency and don't need intense social rituals. The last two need a relational network for their contribution to show.

This cross-mapping is what makes the onboarding model diagnostic rather than cosmetic. It answers one specific question: **given this person's profile, what is the shortest path between their first day at work and their Place of Potential?**

## The cost of ignoring this diversity

Let me close by flipping the question. If organizations keep using a single onboarding model for every profile, what is the cost?

The cost is threefold. For the organization, it is time lost until the new person starts contributing effectively, plus early turnover rates that are usually interpreted as "bad hiring" when, in fact, they are bad integration. For the person, it is the emotional toll of being in an environment that doesn't understand them, and the persistent feeling of getting off on the wrong foot. For the organizational culture, it is the loss of the cognitive diversity that the company, in its discourse, claims to value and, in practice, pushes out the back door.

## Something to reflect on

Think about the onboarding you went through at your current job or your last one. Which elements of that process were coherent with the way you think and coordinate, and which were painful or simply irrelevant to the person you are?

And if you are a manager or work with people, think about the last three hires in your area. Did each of those people get the same onboarding? If so, what is the invisible cost of that standardization? How many of the three would be performing even better, in their third month on the job, if their integration had been designed around who they are, instead of around who the company imagined they would be?
