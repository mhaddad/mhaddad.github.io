Every first-time parent goes through the same vertigo. Every book promises a different method, every grandmother swears her way is best, and every friend has the definitive theory.

And in the middle of all that noise, there's always a sentence with an air of science: "it's been proven that such-and-such is good for the child."

Suppose it really has been proven, that there's serious evidence that giving affectionate attention and responding to crying helps development. Even if that's true, you can draw three very different conclusions from that sentence.

The first honest conclusion is also the most modest: giving affectionate attention is good, period. That's what the research showed, no more and no less.

The second already goes too far, when it concludes that, because of this, that trendy parenting method, which sells an entire system built around the idea, works. But the method depends on much more than one practice, and no one tested the method; they tested the practice.

And there's a third, the one that almost always slips by: that this practice would be exclusive to that method. In fact, strict parents and laid-back parents, with opposite philosophies, give their children affectionate attention all the time.

This example describes, with an almost embarrassing precision, what happens every time people talk about autonomy, self-management and, now, artificial intelligence.

## The leap that enthusiasm hides

Anyone who works with organizational design has seen this scene play out again and again. The director comes back from an event, or has just read an inspiring new book, and arrives with the decision already made: we're going to get rid of hierarchy, because there's research proving that autonomy works.

The first part is a legitimate choice. The second is the parenting-method leap, because between "research shows that autonomy works" and "therefore we must abolish hierarchy" lies the same distance that separates a good parenting practice from a miracle method that promises to raise your children for you.

It's in that distance that a good share of organizational transformations stumble, taking the budget and people's patience down with them.

## The practice has evidence

I'll start with what's solid and backed by research, because it's more than the skeptics imagine.

[Self-Determination Theory](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teoria_da_autodetermina%C3%A7%C3%A3o), by Edward Deci and Richard Ryan, shows the relationship between autonomous motivation and quality work. People tend to engage more, and better, when they act out of their own motivation, and not only under external control.

Amy Edmondson showed, in a 1999 study of 51 teams, that [psychological safety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_safety), the belief that you can take an interpersonal risk without being punished, goes hand in hand with learning behavior.

A [2014 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24188392/) by Wang, Waldman and Zhang brought together dozens of samples and found a positive relationship between shared leadership and team effectiveness. The relationship is stronger with the group's internal processes, such as coordination and trust, than with pure performance.

These are the practices with evidence: autonomy, psychological safety and shared leadership. Each with its own study, its own size and its own reach, and anyone who works with people should know them better than they usually do.

## The method's lack of evidence

Nothing said above proves that a company without bosses performs better, nor that flattening the structure, by itself, improves results. The evidence is about the practices, not about the method someone assembled from them and christened the organization of the future.

The difference is far from academic hair-splitting. Evaluating an isolated practice is relatively simple, but evaluating an entire method, an organization reorganized end to end, would require comparing similar companies, in similar contexts, for long enough, while controlling for the thousand other things that change along with it.

I don't think that study even exists (if it does, let me know). What exists is the inspiring story of a company that succeeded, told by people who have every interest in telling it, and that shows the method is possible, not that it's better or that it will work in your house.

Whoever confuses the two turns a design hypothesis into a promise of salvation. And promises of salvation, in management as in raising children, tend to have a short shelf life.

## The practice isn't exclusive to any method

None of these mechanisms belongs to self-management, because a well-run hierarchy can also generate autonomy, psychological safety and decisions made close to whoever has the information.

Affectionate attention isn't the property of any parenting philosophy, just as autonomy isn't the property of any org chart. The evidence is always about the practice, never about the ideological label stuck on it.

This changes the question worth asking. It stops being "hierarchy or self-management," as if you had to root for a team, and becomes: where, in that specific organization, does it pay to bring decisions closer to the people doing the work, and where does concentrating them make more sense because of risk, legal obligation or the need for clarity?

Autonomy, in this reading, is a design resource to be used where the context makes it worthwhile, not a higher stage of evolution that everyone must reach.

It's a less exciting position than announcing the end of bosses, but a much more honest one. And it ends up giving more authority to those who defend it, because claiming less and backing up what you claim is what separates those who take the subject seriously from those who just switch flags.

## The same chasm, now with AI

This old mistake has put on new clothes, and they're being worn right now, in real time.

There's growing evidence that artificial intelligence improves performance on specific tasks, such as summarizing, classifying, producing a draft or speeding up customer service. These are the practices of the moment, and several of them really do help.

What doesn't exist yet is evidence that reorganizing the entire company around AI produces, by itself, a better organization. That's the method, and the method hasn't been tested.

Between the gain measured on a task and the promise of transforming the company lies exactly the same chasm that separates "autonomy works" from "let's abolish hierarchy." We swapped the practice; the chasm stays the same.

And the detail shows up once again, because the gain from AI doesn't belong to any organizational model either. It doesn't prove that a flat structure works, nor that a hierarchical one does; it only proves that that task, in that context, got better. Whoever turns this into a thesis about the company's organizational design is, once again, confusing the practice with the method.

In the case of AI there's one more irony, because it's often sold as the practice that would finally make the method of the organization without hierarchy viable.

## How to separate the practice from the method

The way out, in all three cases, isn't to dismiss the evidence, but to measure it at the right size.

The next time a study shows up to justify a big decision, whether about autonomy, self-management or AI, it's worth asking two questions.

The first is whether that number speaks of an isolated practice or of the whole method. It almost always speaks of a practice, of a bounded gain, which is already valuable as long as you don't demand more from it than it can sustain.

The second is what else, beyond that practice, needs to be true for things to work in your house. That list is what makes up the real method, made of that child's temperament, that family's history, the specific context.

Carried over to the company, it's your industry, your people and your moment. And that's what decides whether the thing holds up, always as a work of organizational design, never as an act of faith.

## Food for thought

- The last study that convinced you, about autonomy or about AI: did it measure the effect of an isolated practice or the success of an entire method?
- In your organization, which decisions would gain from moving closer to the people doing the work, and which call for concentration because of risk, law or clarity?
- If the practices have evidence but the method doesn't, whose job is it to design the transformation that this organization, with its history and its context, actually needs?
