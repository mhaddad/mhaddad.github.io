In the [previous article](https://medium.com/@mhaddad/quando-uma-pessoa-n%C3%A3o-combina-com-o-seu-trabalho-57661576e96b), I argued that the traditional approach to *Person-Organization Fit*, centered on the congruence of values between individual and organization, has a structural limit: **declared values are poor predictors of actual behavior at work**. The approach I propose is not interested in what a person says they believe, but in how they think, decide and coordinate their own work with others.

This shift requires a theoretical principle to support it. After all, what does it mean, in precise terms, for there to be compatibility between the way someone thinks and what the organization demands of them? That principle is what I call **Cognitive Coherence**.

## A phenomenon that goes beyond work

Before getting to the formal definition, it is worth noting that Cognitive Coherence is not a phenomenon exclusive to the organizational environment. It shows up in any human activity that involves externalizing a thought into some concrete form.

An entrepreneur who conceives a new business does not create a neutral company. They create a company that reflects their worldview, their risk tolerance, their relationship with authority and the way they deal with uncertainty. Two entrepreneurs with the same capital, in the same market, with the same opportunity, build structurally different businesses, because each one operates from a different cognitive architecture.

The same happens in software development. Two programmers solving the same technical problem arrive at different solutions, not because of a difference in competence, but because of a difference in cognitive style: one prefers broad abstractions and generic systems, the other prefers concreteness and domain-specific code. The final solution carries the "cognitive signature" of whoever thought it up.

This phenomenon is also visible in interface design. A designer with a strong appetite for order and hierarchy builds screens with rigid grids, disciplined typography and linear navigation. Another, with greater tolerance for ambiguity, allows overlaps, layers and multiple entry points. Both interfaces may serve the same purpose, but they are different materializations of how each person organizes information in their mind.

Even in seemingly mundane acts, the pattern repeats itself. The structure of a text, the way someone argues, the path chosen to solve a problem, the order in which a person prepares a meal: all of this is, to some extent, the externalization of a particular way of thinking about and processing the world. The recipe is the same, but each person's "kitchen" is unmistakable.

This observation is the key to understanding what happens inside organizations. A company's management model and organizational design are not neutral objects that exist independently of whoever conceived them. They carry the cognitive mark of whoever designed them, and they demand, from whoever will inhabit them, a functional compatibility with that trait.

## Cognitive Coherence: a definition

Formally, I am defining Cognitive Coherence as **the functional compatibility between the individual's cognitive architecture and the organization's architecture of power and communication**.

It is not a static congruence of values, as in the classic *supplementary fit* described by [Kristof-Brown, Schneider and Su (2023)](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2023-62059-001), but **a structural correspondence between how a person processes information, makes decisions and deals with uncertainty**, and what the management and work system demands of them along those same dimensions.

This distinction matters for two reasons.

First, it shifts the object of the diagnosis. Values answer the question "what do you believe in". Cognitive architecture answers the question "how do you operate". The first can be declared in interviews and group exercises. The answer to the second only reveals itself in behavior sustained over time, under real working conditions.

Second, it recognizes that the misalignment between person and organization is not symmetrical. The cognitive and emotional costs of working against your own architecture depend on the direction of the mismatch. A person with high readiness for autonomy placed in a rigid hierarchical structure experiences what could be called **cognitive atrophy**: the chronic underuse of their epistemic capacities, which shows up as boredom, disengagement and eventually turnover. In the opposite direction, a person with low readiness for autonomy placed in a self-managed model experiences **cognitive overload and anxiety**: the absence of a hierarchical reference is experienced as abandonment, and the common adaptive response is to look for informal bosses or for increasingly explicit rules that restore a sense of order.

Neither profile is functionally superior. The dysfunction is not in the people, but in the incoherence between them and the work environment.

## The dimensions of cognitive readiness

For Cognitive Coherence to stop being an intuitive concept and become measurable, I decided to anchor it in empirically validated dimensions. Research on attitudes toward ambiguity provides that anchor. [Lauriola, Foschi, Mosca and Weller (2016)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1073191115577188), in revising the *Multidimensional Attitude toward Ambiguity Scale* (MSTAT-II), identified three robust factors:

1. **Discomfort with Ambiguity (DA)**: Represents the affective dimension of tolerance for uncertainty. It is the emotional response to situations in which expectations are not explicit, roles are fluid and outcomes are unpredictable. People with high discomfort with ambiguity experience sustained anxiety in contexts without processes, without a reference authority or without a clear criterion for what is correct.
2. **Moral Absolutism (MA)**: Represents the cognitive dimension, related to the need for binary categorization of reality. It works as a defense against complexity, reducing multifaceted situations to opposites such as right and wrong, efficient and inefficient. In environments where decisions are built by consent, and rarely have a single answer, this trait becomes a significant source of friction.
3. **Need for Complexity and Novelty (NC)**: Represents the epistemic dimension, which reflects an active preference for multifaceted problems, originality and intellectual curiosity. People with a high need for complexity and novelty see a lack of structure as an opportunity, not as a threat.

The combined configuration of these three factors defines the proposed hypothesis that this P-O Fit model calls **Readiness for Autonomy**: a high need for complexity, low moral absolutism and low discomfort with ambiguity characterize the profile best suited to self-management contexts. The opposite profile operates at maximum effectiveness in command-and-control structures.

To these three dimensions, a fourth construct with an independent foundation is added: Self-Determination Theory ([Deci & Ryan, 2017](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2017-04680-000)), which identifies autonomy as a basic psychological need. [Doblinger (2023)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1198196/full), applying this theory to the study of self-managed organizations, documented what he called a **Critical Autonomy Threshold**: when perceived autonomy exceeds the individual's ideal autonomy, engagement and satisfaction decrease instead of increasing. This finding supports a central point of the model: offering autonomy is not enough; you have to consider each individual's readiness to receive it.

## Learned helplessness

There is yet another phenomenon that extends the reach of Cognitive Coherence beyond stable personality traits. [Valkiainen (2026)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00187267261420797), in ethnographic research on the adoption of Holacracy in self-managed organizations, documented what he called **organizational *learned helplessness***: individuals who spent years in command-and-control structures internalize patterns of deference that make them unable to exercise the authority a new structure formally offers them.

This is not a matter of value incongruence. People affected by learned helplessness often believe in autonomy as a principle. It is a gap between the functional capacity developed over a professional career and the structural demands of the new context. The author identifies three boundary conditions that can break this cycle: **clear boundaries of roles and responsibilities**, **purpose-oriented proactive behavior** and **complex social interaction**. None of them is met by simply redesigning formal structures, which may explain why so many adoptions of self-management fail even when the formal architecture is correctly implemented.

## The creative vector

This last aspect completes the picture. Cognitive Coherence does not operate only from the point of view of the individual who needs to adapt to the organization, but also from the point of view of the organization itself, which is also, ultimately, a cognitive externalization.

When people with a given cognitive architecture occupy positions of influence, such as founders, leaders of cultural transformation or organizational designers, they **tend to create structures that mirror their own mental organization**. Leaders with high tolerance for ambiguity, low moral absolutism and a high need for complexity conceive systems based on autonomy, transparency, distributed governance and continuous learning. Leaders with the opposite profile build rigid hierarchies, sequential processes and redundant supervision mechanisms. In neither case is there bad faith or incompetence. What there is, is the coherent materialization of a way of thinking into an organizational design.

This observation has an important practical implication for P-O Fit diagnosis: **the organization is not a neutral entity to which the employee adapts, but the cognitive product of the people who conceived it**. Assessing compatibility requires understanding not only the individual's profile and the operational demands, but also the cognitive trait crystallized in the company's architecture.

A misalignment between employee and organization may be, in essence, a silent conflict between two distinct cognitive architectures: one alive and present; the other rooted in the design someone left behind in the past.

## Something to reflect on

Think of something you did recently: a text, a presentation, a process, a piece of software, a meal, a career decision… How much of what you produced reflects your particular way of organizing your thinking?

Now widen the question to your professional context. If you look honestly at the organization you work for, how much of the culture, the processes and the way decisions are made there is, in fact, the cognitive externalization of whoever founded or transformed the company? And how much of that organizational architecture is coherent with the way you yourself think?
